So you got fired from your quality startup or your big tech company, and now
the only questions in your head are the ones I had two months ago: what the
hell do we study now, which area is worth betting on, and which tools are good
to go.

This started as a conversation with a friend about how we could both land a
remote job, and it turned into the roadmap I wish someone had handed me. It
organizes my own path, and maybe you leave with a tool you hadn't heard of, or
with confirmation for one you had.

## Where I'm speaking from

I started searching two months ago. Right now I have four offers, most of them
from consultancies. I also got deep into two product companies but didn't pass
their last technical round, mainly because system design is my weak spot, and
that round usually sits right before the behavioural. I have five years of
full-stack experience.

I did competitive programming, so algorithms are the comfortable part: I can
analyze and present a solution for pretty much any LeetCode medium. Hards are
another story. They combine several patterns under tight constraints,
sometimes with an advanced structure on top, and while big tech runs mostly on
mediums too, the hards show up in their onsites and senior loops. I decided
that grind wasn't for me this round.

Two things are non-negotiable wherever you apply. English, and understanding
the business: in every interview you should know what they're building and ask
good questions about it.

The third thing changed since my last job hunt: being a builder matters now.
They want to see design principles, that you can work with AI, and that you
communicate well. You need to sell what you build, and sell yourself as the
person who built it. The AI part is big enough that it gets its own section
below.

## Before any meeting

Polish your LinkedIn first. There's plenty of advice out there; what worked
for me:

- A generic but clean cover image.
- A profile photo that looks professional.
- A shorter headline with strong words.
- A portfolio. Mine is simple on purpose, but a good one can also show your
  design taste.
- Experience and personal projects actually up to date.

## The stages

Almost every process I went through looked like this:

1. HR screen
2. Technical interview (async or sync)
3. System design interview
4. A meeting with a manager, a founder, sometimes the CEO

Two footnotes from the trenches. Outside big tech, step 3 is often not a
formal system design round: at consultancies and small product companies it
becomes a technical-depth conversation, or gets merged into the final meeting.
And if the consultancy does staff augmentation, expect a bonus round where the
client interviews you again after you already passed everything. The
behavioural questions rarely get their own stage; they're sprinkled into the
HR screen and the final meeting.

## The HR screen

My notes call this stage RRHH, because Spanish. Your recruiter calls it HR.

Here you need to convince a person that you're a good match for the role.
Explain things simply. Communication is everything.

What works for me is preparing answers before the questions arrive. They
usually follow from the role, so ask an AI what's likely to come up and do
your own research on top. Then there are the evergreens: the last thing you
built and how, your current AI workflow, the most challenging thing you've
done, your biggest mistake in production, the next thing you want to learn.

I keep a tested file of answers that I know sound like me. Think of it as the
script for a play where you're a famous enough actor to improvise: you can
change the lines, but you'd better know the play.

My rules for this stage:

- Don't blame others in your answers. Own what you did.
- When a story involves asking a more experienced teammate for help, say so.
  You can't know everything, and solo players have a bad reputation.
- Your AI workflow can't be "I ask Claude and turn on plan mode." Research
  real ways of working with AI. More on this below.

## The technical interview

Usually the second step. Sometimes it's the first. Whatever, you prepare the
same way.

My advice: practice one language until it's boring, and if they propose
another, ask whether you can use yours. There's too much to learn in 2026 to
maintain two interview languages, and most companies let you pick any
mainstream one anyway. The exceptions are domain rounds: a front-end interview
will be JavaScript, a mobile one speaks whatever the platform speaks, and
consultancy tests are sometimes tied to the client's stack.

For practice I recommend LeetCode or any platform like it, though I prefer
[NeetCode](https://neetcode.io/): the curated lists (the NeetCode 150 is free)
give you structure instead of a pile of thousands of unsorted problems. I run
one of those study plans as fast as I can, and in the process I only use AI to
ask how a solution could be more performant or which more advanced concept
applies. Not to solve the problem for me.

The data structures you should be able to build and use:

- Arrays -> static and dynamic, and how they differ under the hood.
- Queue and stack -> the classic Valid Parentheses problem teaches a pattern
  you'll reuse everywhere.
- Linked lists -> easy to understand, and reversing one is the canonical
  warm-up:

```ts
type ListNode = { val: number; next: ListNode | null };

function reverse(head: ListNode | null): ListNode | null {
  let prev: ListNode | null = null;
  while (head) {
    const next = head.next; // save it before we cut the link
    head.next = prev;
    prev = head;
    head = next;
  }
  return prev;
}
```

- Hash tables -> must know. No negotiation on this one.
- Binary trees -> good to know, and the exercises are genuinely fun.
- B-trees -> good to know conceptually. Nearly every mainstream database
  (Postgres, MySQL, SQLite, MongoDB) uses the B-tree family (usually B+
  trees) for its indexes, and often for the table data itself. Nobody will
  ask you to implement one; they'll ask why an index makes a query fast, and
  when it doesn't help.
- Tries -> autocomplete. Elasticsearch's completion suggester runs on
  trie-shaped automata, so this one is not just interview folklore.
- Probabilistic structures -> Bloom filters (RocksDB and Cassandra use them
  to skip files that can't contain your key), HyperLogLog, count-min sketch.
  Honesty: outside big tech these live in system design conversations, not
  coding rounds.

And at minimum these algorithms:

- Recursion.
- DFS and BFS.
- Binary search, and not just the code: understand why it works.

I've written a longer [field guide to these structures](/blog/data-structures-typescript-python)
and a whole post on [binary search beyond arrays](/blog/binary-search-on-the-answer)
if you want to go deeper.

## System design and CEO talks

Honesty first: this is the round that knocked me out of two product companies,
so read this section as notes from the guy who failed it, not the guy who
passed it.

One clarification so you don't repeat my typo: the round is called system
design. Architecture, APIs, database choices, scaling, trade-offs. A design
system is a different thing entirely, the frontend artifact with component
libraries and design tokens, and some frontend loops test that separately.

What I can recommend: research the team you'd be joining and what they're
building, so the conversation can go toward the systems they actually run
instead of a generic URL shortener.

For learning the material itself, I recommend Matt Pocock's /teach skill
(`npx skills add mattpocock/skills --skill=teach`). It turns a directory into
a study workspace your coding agent teaches from, pulling cited sources
instead of answering from model memory.

The final meeting with the manager or CEO doubles as the behavioural round, so
your HR-screen script gets a second performance.

## The AI workflow question

In 2026 your AI workflow is part of the interview. Canva replaced its
computer-science-fundamentals round with an AI-assisted coding interview. Meta
added an AI-enabled round, Google is piloting one, and AI-native startups like
Sierra hand you agent tooling and watch what you do with it.

What they evaluate is not the size of your rig. It's judgment: when you
rejected AI output, how you knew it was wrong, how you validate what your
agents produce. "I ask Claude and turn on plan mode" dies at the first
follow-up question. A practiced setup, rules files, agents, a repeatable
workflow you can rebuild in front of someone, helps you demo that judgment
convincingly. But the setup is the prop; the review habits are the act.

One caveat before you show up with your rig: some companies still ban AI in
interviews entirely, Amazon and Goldman among them. Check before you plug in.
