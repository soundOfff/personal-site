I'd spent three and a half years making backends that didn't fall over. Then I
joined a startup, and learned that not falling over was the easy part.

Before [Dex](/work/dex) I worked at scale. A few million users, e-commerce
traffic that spiked on payday weekends and never apologised. The problems there
were large but they were never _vague_: you knew the shape of the load, you knew
which path could never lie, and there was enough structure around you that a
careful engineer could be careful. I spent those years getting good at one
thing — making a system stay correct under pressure — and I quietly assumed
that was most of the job.

Dex was my first real startup, and the first thing it taught me is that the
structure I'd been taking for granted was load-bearing.

## Chaos after structure

A startup at speed is a different climate, not just a faster version of the same
one. The roadmap was a guess that got re-rolled every couple of weeks. The thing
that was urgent on Monday was sometimes irrelevant by Thursday, because a
customer call or a cost spike had rewritten the priorities underneath us. I wore
more hats in my first month than in the previous three years combined —
backend, a bit of infra, a bit of product, a lot of "whoever notices it owns
it."

That sounds like a complaint. It isn't. It was the most I'd ever learned in a
fixed stretch of time, precisely because nothing waited for me to feel ready.

## Learning on the clock

I came in as a backend engineer. The feature I was handed was an **LLM**
feature, and I had never put a model into production in my life.

Three and a half years of experience, and a lot of it didn't transfer cleanly.
Row-level locks and idempotency keys tell you nothing about how to make a model
call cheap, or how to know whether a smaller model is "good enough," or what
happens to your product when someone else's API has a bad night. I had to learn
a new domain quickly — in public, against real traffic, with the meter
running — because the alternative was the feature not shipping.

And the feature demoed beautifully. That was the trap.

## The middle is the product

Every AI product demos well now. You type a prompt, the model answers, the room
nods. The demo dazzled me too, at first — until the bill arrived and I
understood that the part everyone had been admiring was the part we hadn't
built. The model was rented. The floor on demo quality was set by someone
else's last training run, not by anything our team had done.

Everything that turned that demo into a product lived somewhere much less
photogenic: routing, rate limits, evals, fallbacks, cost ceilings. **The
unglamorous middle.** Nobody puts it in the deck. It turned out to be the job —
and, eventually, the place I was most useful.

The feature had started where most LLM features start: one model, the biggest
tier, on every request — the "pull the date out of this email" requests and the
"reason through this contract" requests alike. So the bill scaled linearly with
usage, and the better the feature did, the worse it looked in the cost review.

The fix wasn't clever. It was a router that classified each prompt and walked a
catalog ordered cheapest-to-dearest, taking the first model that cleared the
bar:

```ts
for (const entry of CATALOG) {
  if (entry.clears.includes(cls) && env[entry.keyName]) return entry;
}
```

A few lines. The work was never those lines — it was earning the word
_clears_. A bar set too low ships quality regressions one cheap call at a time;
a bar set too high means everything escalates and you never see the savings. So
most of my real effort went into writing honest per-class evals and keeping them
green while the models and prompts shifted underneath. The routing logic was an
afternoon. The contracts that made it trustworthy were the quarter.

The rest of the middle was the same texture. A cap before a launch — no
anonymous caller should be able to spend an unbounded amount of someone else's
money. A fallback, so a provider's bad night degraded the product instead of
taking it down. None of it was novel. All of it was the difference between a
thing that demos and a thing that survives week two.

## What the startup taught me that scale didn't

At my old job I learned to find the one path that must never lie, make it small,
and let everything else be eventually consistent. That instinct still serves me.

But the startup taught me two things scale never had to. The first is that you
can pick up an entire unfamiliar domain faster than you'd believe when there's
no one else to hand it to and the feature ships either way — competence under a
deadline is mostly just refusing to wait until you feel qualified. The second is
that the work users actually pay for is almost never the work that looks
impressive in a demo. It's the answer that's still there at 2 a.m., the bill
that surprises nobody, the quality that's the same on Thursday as it was on
Monday.

That's not the boring part around the product.

For me, three and a half years in and finally in a room moving fast enough to
prove it, it was the product.
