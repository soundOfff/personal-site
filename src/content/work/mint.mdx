> **Narrative only.** Mint is employer work, so this case study is a written account
> with no proprietary code, and figures are directional rather than exact.

## Problem

E-commerce APIs serving a few million users have no quiet hours. Traffic spikes —
campaign drops, payday weekends, flash promotions — are the normal case, not the
exception, and the hard part isn't raw throughput. It's staying **correct under
contention**: inventory that never oversells, carts that never lose items, checkout
that never double-charges, all while the read side absorbs orders of magnitude more
traffic than the write side.

## Architecture

The shape that worked was an aggressive separation of the read and write paths
around a single Postgres source of truth.

- **Reads got cached, writes got queued.** Catalog and pricing — the overwhelming
  majority of traffic — served from a cache layer in front of Postgres, with
  invalidation driven by write events rather than TTL guesswork on the hot paths.
- **The inventory path stayed boringly transactional.** Stock decrements ran as
  short, row-level-locked transactions with explicit lock ordering. No clever
  distributed counters; one database, correct by construction.
- **Checkout was made idempotent end to end.** Every order submission carried an
  idempotency key, so a retried request — mobile networks retry constantly — could
  never create a second order or a second charge.
- **The slow stuff left the request path.** Emails, invoices, search indexing, and
  analytics moved behind queues, keeping the synchronous path down to "validate,
  reserve, charge, respond".

## The key tradeoff

Consistency versus latency on the inventory path. Serving stock levels from cache
makes product pages fast but lies during a rush; locking rows on every read makes
them honest but slow. We split the difference deliberately: **product pages show
cached, slightly-stale stock; checkout re-validates against the source of truth
inside the transaction.** A shopper might occasionally see "in stock" on an item
that sells out seconds later — but nobody ever pays for an item the warehouse
doesn't have. Choosing _where_ the staleness is allowed to live, rather than
pretending it away, was the decision the whole system leaned on.

## Outcome

The platform rode its peak campaigns — multiples of baseline traffic — without
oversells and without checkout incidents, and tail latency on the buying path
stayed flat as traffic grew. The deeper outcome was operational: because
correctness lived in a small, well-locked core instead of being smeared across
services, scaling work became cache and queue tuning — routine, low-risk changes —
rather than surgery on the money path.

The habit Mint drilled in — find the one path that must never lie, make it small,
and let everything else be eventually consistent — is the same instinct behind
[the router's blast-radius cap](/work/flagship): decide your worst case up front
and make it structural.
